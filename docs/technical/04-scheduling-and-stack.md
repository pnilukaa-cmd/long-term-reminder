3. **Local storage: `drift`** (type-safe SQL over `sqflite`) — §4.
4. **Billing: direct Play Billing via Flutter's official `in_app_purchase` plugin, not RevenueCat.** This **overturns** the prior-research-favored default recorded in `CLAUDE.md`. Reasoning in §5.

## 1. Do these reminders need exact alarms at all?

**No.** Reasoning, grounded in Android's own documentation:

### What "exact" actually buys you, and why this app doesn't need it

Android distinguishes exact alarms (`setExactAndAllowWhileIdle()`, `setAlarmClock()` — require the `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` permission) from inexact ones (`setAndAllowWhileIdle()`, `setWindow()`, or `WorkManager` with an initial delay — no special permission required). **[Android docs]** Per *"Schedule exact alarms"* (developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/alarms/schedule-exact-alarms): starting with apps targeting Android 14 (API 34), `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` is **no longer granted by default at install** for general-purpose apps — the user must explicitly grant it via a Settings redirect (Alarms & Reminders). A narrow "calendar/alarm-clock" exemption (`USE_EXACT_ALARM`) exists, but Play policy restricts that declaration to apps whose *core function* is an alarm clock or a calendar app with user-facing exact-time events — a renewal reminder app does not qualify, and declaring it without qualifying risks a Play policy rejection, which is itself a reason to avoid this path unless the product genuinely needs it.

This app's own cadence design already establishes it doesn't: per `docs/design/02-v1-design.md` §2, the coarsest ladder stage is 6 months, the finest is 1 day, and every acceptance criterion in `docs/requirements/03-v1-acceptance-criteria.md` is written against "the reminder fires on [date]," never "at [time]." The product's own notification copy (`REQ-11.3`: `"[Label] · due in [N] days"`) never promises a specific hour. There is no requirement anywhere in this project that needs minute- or hour-precision delivery — the entire premise of exact alarms (guaranteed exact-time firing through Doze) is solving a problem this app doesn't have.

### What inexact scheduling actually guarantees

**[Android docs]** Two credible primitives, both usable without the exact-alarm permission:

- **`AlarmManager.setAndAllowWhileIdle()` / `setWindow()`** — the app requests a wall-clock (`RTC_WAKEUP`) delivery time; the system is allowed to defer delivery into upcoming Doze maintenance windows but is *not* allowed to defer indefinitely — these calls exist specifically so non-exempt apps have a "close enough" path through Doze. No special permission required.
- **`WorkManager`** (one-time `OneTimeWorkRequest` with an initial delay) — per *"Guaranteed execution"* / WorkManager overview docs, WorkManager's own stated contract is *"the work is guaranteed to run, but exact timing is not guaranteed"* — it explicitly recommends `AlarmManager` instead for anything genuinely time-sensitive. WorkManager is the right tool for the *reconciliation/self-heal* job (§2), not for firing the user-facing notification itself.

**[Android docs]** Per *"Optimize for Doze and App Standby"* (developer.android.com/training/monitoring-device-state/doze-standby): during deep Doze, the system still opens periodic maintenance windows (frequency backs off the longer the device stays idle, but does not stop entirely), during which `setAndAllowWhileIdle()`-class alarms and pending jobs are allowed to fire. **[Android docs]** Per *App Standby Buckets* (developer.android.com/topic/performance/appstandby): an app's usage pattern buckets it into `active`/`working_set`/`frequent`/`rare`/`restricted`, which throttles how often the system opportunistically wakes the app for jobs — this is the more realistic risk for an app a user might only open a few times a month by design (its entire pitch is "you don't need to open it"), and it's the specific reason self-heal (§2) needs a belt-and-suspenders mechanism rather than relying on a single scheduled call.

### What could realistically slip, and whether it matters

Realistic worst-case slippage under this design is measured in **hours, not days**, for an actively-scheduled device — Doze maintenance windows recur multiple times per day even in deep idle. The one scenario where slippage could plausibly cross a full calendar day is a device that's aggressively bucketed as `rare`/`restricted` *and* an OEM battery manager on top of stock Android further suppresses background work (§2). Given every acceptance criterion in this project is written at day granularity, a few hours of slippage doesn't break anything. The one genuinely sensitive case is a ladder's **final "1 day before" stage** (Insurance, Warranty, Custom-Short) — if that specific notification slipped by more than 24 hours, it could in theory land after the due date has already passed. This doesn't break any acceptance criterion either (REQ-3.3's Day-0 overdue nag exists precisely to catch this), but it's worth a defensive choice: **schedule final-stage notifications for the morning of their target day** (not evening), giving slippage room to still land same-day before treating it as a non-issue.

**What would falsify this and require escalating to exact alarms:** if real-device testing (§6) shows delivery routinely slipping past the same calendar day under realistic (not artificially compressed) Doze/standby conditions — not the clock-jump test, which validates something different (§6, item 6) — that's the signal to revisit. If that happens, the fallback isn't "turn on exact alarms for everything" — it would be requesting `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` only for the Day-0/final-stage notifications specifically, which is a real user-facing permission gate and a real scope addition (`05-permissions.html` panels p4–p6 come back into the build), not a silent flip of a flag. I don't expect this outcome based on documented Android behavior, but it's a named, falsifiable claim, not a hand-wave.

**Recommendation:** cut the exact-alarm permission flow (`05-permissions.html` p4–p6) from the v1 build, exactly as the design doc's own "zero rework if unneeded" framing anticipated. This also avoids the Play Console "Alarms & Reminders" permission-declaration/justification step entirely — one less thing to defend during Play review.

## 2. What survives reboot, app update, force-stop, and OEM battery optimization?

This is the more important engineering problem of the two, and where the real design work is. A reminder six months out that silently never fires is the worst possible failure for this product — worse than no app, because the user was relying on it. The architecture below is built around **making the exact answer to "did this survive X" not matter**, because it self-heals on every plausible re-entry point, rather than trying to guarantee any single scheduling call survives everything.

### What's documented to break, and why a single scheduling call isn't enough

- **Reboot [Android docs]:** alarms registered directly via `AlarmManager` do **not** survive a device reboot unless the app explicitly re-registers them — normally via a `RECEIVE_BOOT_COMPLETED` broadcast receiver. `WorkManager`, by contrast, persists its own work requests to an internal database and re-arms itself after reboot automatically, without the app needing its own boot receiver — this is a documented, real advantage of routing the *reconciliation* job through WorkManager rather than raw `AlarmManager`.
- **Force-stop [Android docs, high confidence but verify current wording at implementation time]:** Android's `AlarmManager` documentation states that as of Android 12, if the user (or an OEM "kill app" action that maps to force-stop) force-stops an app, **all of that app's pending alarms and scheduled jobs are cancelled, and the app cannot schedule new background work until the user manually relaunches it.** This is not a bug to route around — it's Android's own designed behavior, and there is no technical workaround. The only mitigation is: the moment the user does reopen the app, everything gets fixed (self-heal-on-launch, below) — and product-level guidance not to force-stop the app should live in Settings/help copy, since this is a real, self-inflicted failure mode a user could trigger without understanding the consequence.
- **App update [assumption / needs device verification]:** I could not find an authoritative, current Android statement guaranteeing that `AlarmManager` registrations survive an APK update (as opposed to a reboot). `WorkManager` work requests are documented to survive updates as long as worker class names are unchanged. Rather than resolve this by assumption, the architecture below is built so it doesn't matter which way this goes — reconciliation runs regardless.
- **OEM battery optimization [community-documented, not an Android guarantee]:** Samsung, Xiaomi, OnePlus, Huawei, and others ship battery-management layers on top of stock Android (e.g., Samsung's "Sleeping apps"/"Deep sleep" lists, MIUI's aggressive autostart restrictions) that go beyond what Doze/App Standby do, and are well-documented by the community effort at `dontkillmyapp.com` (not a Google source, but a credible, widely-cited aggregation of per-OEM behavior). These can suppress background work even when the app has correctly used stock Doze-compatible APIs. **This is the one category of risk architecture genuinely cannot fully solve** — it needs real-device verification (§6) and, if it turns out to bite, user-facing guidance pointing at the specific OEM's battery settings for that device.

### The design: self-healing reconciliation, not a single trusted scheduling call

1. **Source of truth lives in the local database** (`drift`, §4), not in the OS alarm table. A `scheduled_notifications` table records, per item and ladder/overdue stage: `fire_at`, `android_notification_id`, and a status of `pending` / `fired` / `cancelled` / `skipped`.
2. **Each pending stage is armed via `flutter_local_notifications`'s `zonedSchedule`** with `AndroidScheduleMode.inexactAllowWhileIdle` — the direct Flutter equivalent of `setAndAllowWhileIdle()`. Notifications for a shared calendar day carry a common `groupKey`, giving `NotificationCompat` group-summary behavior (REQ-12.1/12.2) for free.
3. **A reconciliation function is the actual source of correctness**, run at two points:
   - **On every app launch/foreground** — recomputes, from item + entitlement data, exactly which notifications *should* be pending, diffs against the `scheduled_notifications` table, re-arms anything missing, and cancels anything that shouldn't exist (covers edit/delete side effects per REQ-16, and covers the "already-fired notifications never re-fire" half of REQ-17.2, since `fired` status is never re-armed).
   - **On a periodic background pass**, via the `workmanager` Flutter plugin's persisted `PeriodicWorkRequest` (e.g., once every 24h — well above the 15-minute Android minimum for periodic work, no need to push that limit). Because `WorkManager` persists and re-arms itself across reboot **[Android docs]**, this single mechanism gives reboot-survival without a hand-written native `BOOT_COMPLETED` receiver, and gives update-survival insurance regardless of how that ambiguity resolves.
   - **Clock-jump-forward handling (REQ-17.2)** falls directly out of this design: reconciliation marks any `pending` stage whose `fire_at` is now in the past as `skipped`, never re-armed immediately — this is the same code path that prevents a burst of stale notifications after a clock correction, not a special case bolted on.
4. **The one gap nothing above closes**: a force-stopped app's periodic `WorkManager` task is *also* cancelled and won't run until the user reopens the app — per Android's own documented force-stop behavior, above. This is stated plainly rather than implied away: **if a user force-stops the app and doesn't reopen it before a due date passes, the reminder silently doesn't fire, and no technical fix exists for that inside Android's model.** It's the same category of irreducible risk as OEM killers. The honest mitigation is (a) self-heal instantly repairs everything the moment the user does reopen the app — no permanent data loss, no orphaned state — and (b) this should be named explicitly in Settings/help copy ("don't force-stop this app if you want reminders to keep working"), not silently hoped around.

This directly satisfies REQ-16.1/16.2 (edit/delete reschedule bookkeeping) and REQ-17.2 (timezone/clock-change handling) as a side effect of the same mechanism, not as separate logic — which also partially de-risks BA's flagged risk item 16.4/§16.2 concern about notification-ID bookkeeping complexity (§0's `scheduled_notifications` table is exactly the single source of truth that concern was asking for).

## 3. Confirm or overturn Flutter

**Confirmed, not overturned.** The `flutter create` scaffold already on the user's machine is a data point, not a deciding factor — it's genuinely about a minute of work either way, and I'm not treating it as sunk cost.

**Why Flutter holds up for the part of this app that's actually hard:**

- The hard technical core, per §1–§2, is entirely Android OS-level scheduling behavior — `AlarmManager` scheduling modes, `WorkManager` persistence, notification grouping, boot/force-stop/Doze semantics. `flutter_local_notifications` is a thin, mature wrapper that exposes these primitives directly (its `AndroidScheduleMode` enum maps 1:1 onto `setExactAndAllowWhileIdle`/`setAndAllowWhileIdle`/etc.; its notification builder exposes `groupKey`/`setAsGroupSummary` directly onto `NotificationCompat` grouping). Flutter isn't abstracting away or hiding the behavior that matters here — it's calling the same APIs native Kotlin would call, through a package with a large install base and years of real-world use against exactly this scheduling surface. There is no meaningful "Flutter tax" on the riskiest part of this build.
- **"Built with AI assistance" is a real, if soft, argument in Flutter's favor here, not just a neutral fact.** Flutter/Dart plus `flutter_local_notifications` + `workmanager` + `drift` + `in_app_purchase` is an extremely well-represented app shape in general training data and package documentation. Hand-written native Kotlin `AlarmManager`/`WorkManager`/`Room`/Compose plumbing has more surface area for exactly the kind of subtle, hard-to-notice mistakes this app can't afford to make silently — wrong `PendingIntent` mutability flags (a real, commonly-hit Android 12+ footgun), a missing boot receiver manifest entry, an incorrectly scoped notification channel. A well-trodden Flutter package smooths over more of that than hand-rolled native code would, for a solo AI-assisted build.
- v1 is Android-only, so Flutter's headline cross-platform benefit isn't being spent yet — that's a real, honest point in native's favor, not dismissed here. But the project's own framing keeps an iOS port "open... not v1" (`CLAUDE.md`), and if that ever happens, the scheduling architecture above ports conceptually with minimal rework (iOS local notifications have their own, different reliability model, but the app-level reconciliation pattern is platform-agnostic) — a real, if secondary, argument for not closing that door for zero present benefit.
- **The genuine cost of Flutter here is dependency-freshness risk**, concentrated on exactly the subsystem that matters most: `flutter_local_notifications` has, historically, had real compatibility bumps around major Android permission-model changes (12/13's notification-permission and alarm-permission shifts were exactly this kind of moment). This is a real, named risk, not hidden — it's the thing that should be checked (current maintenance status, changelog for API 34/35 compatibility) at the moment implementation actually starts, since package status can shift between this decision and build start.

**What would change my mind:** (a) if, at build-start, `flutter_local_notifications` or `workmanager` show signs of being unmaintained or incompatible with the current target Android API level — a concrete, checkable fact at implementation time, not knowable from here; (b) if real-device testing (§6) surfaces a *Flutter-specific* scheduling defect that a native `AlarmManager`/`WorkManager` implementation wouldn't have (as opposed to a general Android platform limitation that would affect native code identically); (c) if `in_app_purchase`'s Play Billing wrapper (§5) turns out to lag behind a Play Billing Library version Google is about to deprecate. None of these are true today as far as I can establish without live access to pub.dev/current package state — flagging as a pre-build sanity check, not a decision blocker.

## 4. Local storage choice

**`drift`** (a type-safe SQL toolkit over `sqflite`/`sqlite3`), not raw `sqflite`, and not a NoSQL store (Hive/Isar).

**Reasoning:** the data is genuinely relational and the correctness-critical part of this app is exactly the kind of thing a real schema with real queries protects: items ↔ their computed `scheduled_notifications` rows (§2), with reconciliation logic that needs to reliably diff "what's in the DB" against "what the OS thinks is scheduled." BA's risk item 16.2 (`docs/requirements/03-v1-acceptance-criteria.md` §16.2) names exactly this bookkeeping as the single most state-machine-heavy part of the build — a compile-time-checked SQL layer with real transactions is the right tool for getting that bookkeeping right, over hand-written raw-SQL string queries (`sqflite` alone) or a schemaless object store that would make "which notification IDs belong to which item, which are pending vs. fired vs. cancelled" harder to reason about and query correctly. `drift` is actively maintained and widely used in the Flutter ecosystem specifically for this shape of local-only app.

A small number of one-time flags (Health check note dismissed, first-launch state) can live in the same `drift` database as a tiny settings table rather than introducing a second storage mechanism (e.g., `shared_preferences`) for no real benefit — one source of truth, one thing to reason about.

## 5. RevenueCat vs. Play Billing directly

**Direct Play Billing, via Flutter's official `in_app_purchase` plugin — not RevenueCat.** This **overturns** the prior-research-favored default carried in `CLAUDE.md` ("Prior research... points toward... RevenueCat for a one-time unlock — but none of that is a decision yet, and `developer` should confirm or overturn it rather than inherit it uncritically"). I'm overturning it.

**Reasoning:**

- RevenueCat's real value proposition is unifying entitlement state and subscription-lifecycle complexity (proration, grace periods, webhooks, cross-store reconciliation) across multiple platforms and purchase types. This app has **one SKU, one-time non-consumable, one platform (Android, v1)** — none of the complexity RevenueCat exists to absorb is present yet.
- Google Play requires all in-app digital purchases to route through Play Billing regardless of RevenueCat — RevenueCat sits *on top of* Play Billing, it doesn't replace the requirement. Choosing direct integration removes a layer and a third-party account/API-key dependency, not a Play requirement.
- `in_app_purchase` is the official Flutter-team-maintained plugin wrapping Play Billing Library directly, with `restorePurchases()`/`queryPurchases()` covering REQ-14.3's restore flow natively — no third-party service in the loop.
- **This doesn't fully solve the privacy-copy tension REQ-15.2 already flagged**, and I want to be precise about that rather than imply it does: BA correctly flagged that *any* entitlement-verification service that calls out to a network — RevenueCat or Google's own Play Billing servers — makes the Settings privacy card's "never leave your phone" copy not literally true for purchase/entitlement data, even though item data itself stays fully local. Direct Play Billing has a smaller network surface (one fewer third party, no RevenueCat account/API key), but the same REQ-15.2 copy revision is still needed either way. Routed back to business-analyst/product-manager: the privacy card copy needs a scoped exception ("except the one-time purchase check, handled directly by Google Play") regardless of which billing approach ships.
- **When this could flip back:** if an iOS port actually happens post-v1, RevenueCat's cross-platform entitlement sync becomes genuinely valuable and worth reconsidering — not before.

## 6. Device-verification checklist

This environment cannot run any of this — no device, emulator, or SDK exists here. Everything below is written to be run on the user's real device, and is immediately actionable since a physical device is already available. Each item states what to do, how, and what result would falsify the design in §1–§2.

Assumed package name for adb commands below: replace `<pkg>` with the actual applicationId once the project is set up (e.g., `com.example.renewal_reminder`).

| # | Test | How | Falsifies the design if... |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Basic inexact delivery | Schedule a stage for ~2 minutes out, screen off, device untouched. Confirm it fires. | Doesn't fire at all within a few minutes of screen-off idle. |
| 2 | **Forced Doze delivery** | `adb shell dumpsys battery unplug`, then `adb shell dumpsys deviceidle force-idle` to push the device into Doze immediately **[documented adb command, "Test your app with Doze and App Standby," developer.android.com]**. With a stage scheduled ~15–30 min out, leave the device in forced Doze and watch for delivery (check `adb shell dumpsys deviceidle` for the next maintenance window). | 5 | **Force-stop recovery** | `adb shell am force-stop <pkg>` (or Settings → Apps → force stop). Confirm `dumpsys alarm` shows nothing for `<pkg>`. Reopen the app manually and confirm self-heal-on-launch repopulates the full expected schedule (re-check `dumpsys alarm`). | Reopening the app does **not** restore the full correct schedule — this is the one case the architecture explicitly can't prevent, only repair; the repair itself must work. |
| 6 | **App update survival** | Build and install v1, schedule items, then build/install an update over it (`adb install -r`) without uninstalling. Check `dumpsys alarm` before and after. | This is the one genuinely unresolved documented-fact gap (§2) — run this specifically to settle it, not assume either outcome. |
| 7 | **OEM battery management (the real device's actual manufacturer)** | Locate the OEM's own battery-optimization setting for the app (Samsung: "Put unused apps to sleep" / battery details; Xiaomi/MIUI: autostart + battery saver; OnePlus: battery optimization list — whichever applies to the device on hand). Test with default OEM settings vs. the app manually exempted. | Reminders fail with default OEM settings even though stock-Android Doze/standby tests (2–3) pass — confirms this is a real residual risk needing Settings-copy guidance, not an architecture bug. |
| 8 | **Compressing the 6-month wait — two separate tricks, don't conflate them** | **(a) For realistic Doze/standby delivery timing**, don't manipulate the clock — schedule a short-horizon item (due tomorrow, or use a raw test stage a few minutes out) and use tests 2–3 above; artificially jumping the clock doesn't exercise the real idle timer. **(b) For REQ-17.2's clock-jump-forward behavior specifically**, this is the one place manipulating the clock is the correct test: create an item due ~6 months out, disable automatic date/time (Settings → System → Date & time), and jump the device clock forward in large steps (e.g., month by month). Confirm skipped-over stages are marked `skipped` (not burst-fired) and only currently-due stages behave correctly relative to the new "now." | For (a): notification slips past the same calendar day under realistic short-horizon Doze cycling. For (b): jumping the clock forward causes multiple stale notifications to fire in a burst — this is the exact regression REQ-17.2 exists to prevent, and this test is the cheapest way to catch it without waiting six months. |

**Net falsification signal for the whole design:** if tests 2, 3, or 8a show delivery routinely slipping past the same calendar day under realistic (non-artificial) conditions on stock Android — not an OEM overlay issue (test 7), which is a separately named, already-accepted residual risk — that's the trigger to escalate to exact alarms for the Day-0/final-stage notifications specifically, per the fallback path named in §1, not to silently work around it.
